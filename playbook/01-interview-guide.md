# Church Website Discovery Interview Guide

## Recommended format

Use two 90-minute recorded interviews and one 30–45 minute verification session as a planning default, not a rigid program. The core interview works best with one facilitator and one or two church representatives who can speak for both identity and operations. Invite a specialist only for the relevant section when necessary.

This file is the **master question library**. The human facilitator normally uses a shorter Conversation Brief generated from current project state, existing-site findings, prior transcripts, and uncovered gaps. A person may begin with no preparation beyond knowing the church and the reason for the project.

Do not treat the guide as a questionnaire to rush through. Ask the main question, listen for the subject's own language, then use the probes to make the answer complete and unambiguous.

## How a dynamic interview works

Each topic has four layers:

1. **Opening question** — broad enough for the participant to answer naturally.
2. **Listen-for cues** — facts, decisions, distinctions, and language the facilitator should notice.
3. **Optional probes** — used only when the answer leaves a meaningful gap.
4. **Completion test** — the information needed before the subject may be marked covered.

The Conversation Brief should contain 5–8 opening questions for a typical session. It may include concise probes, but it should not expose the facilitator to every possible sub-question at once.

### Priority order for choosing the next questions

1. Facts required for safety, accuracy, or launch.
2. Unknowns that affect the sitemap or project scope.
3. Contradictions that affect public claims.
4. Questions needed to understand visitor fit and next steps.
5. Details needed for page copy.
6. Optional depth and future-phase material.

If an existing site already answers a question clearly and the information appears current, the interview should verify it rather than ask the participant to recreate it from scratch.

## Before recording

Confirm:

- permission to record and transcribe;
- each speaker's name, role, and approval authority;
- whether any part of the conversation is private;
- who can resolve theological, operational, or technical questions later.

### Opening script

> We are creating source material for your website. I will ask you to describe the church in your own words and explain how things actually work today. We may also discuss hopes or recommendations, but we will label those separately. If something is uncertain, say so; we would rather create a follow-up question than fill a gap. We will organize and polish what you say, but nothing becomes an approved public claim until you review it.

## Follow-up probes to use throughout

Use these whenever an answer is vague:

- Is that true today, or is it something you hope to become?
- Can you give a concrete example?
- What would a first-time visitor actually see or experience?
- Is that the church's approved language or your personal wording?
- Is this safe and appropriate to publish?
- Who owns this information and can confirm it?
- Does this ever vary by service, campus, age group, or season?
- What should happen next after someone reads this?
- Is there an existing document, link, or system that is the official source?

## Session A — Identity, audience, and voice

### 1. Project context

1. Are we launching a new site, redesigning the whole site, or improving selected areas?
2. Why are you doing this now?
3. What is not working about the current website or process?
4. What must remain unchanged?
5. What would make church leaders call this project successful six months after launch?
6. Who approves facts, theology, design, and final launch?

For a redesign, ask:

- Which current pages or features are valuable?
- What do visitors or members repeatedly struggle to find?
- What content is outdated, duplicated, embarrassing, or burdensome to maintain?
- Are there URLs, archives, downloads, or rankings that must be preserved?
- What do analytics, search queries, or form submissions reveal, if anything?

### 2. Audience and outcomes

1. Who most needs this website?
2. If both newcomers and members matter, which group takes priority when their needs compete?
3. Describe the kinds of first-time visitors you commonly receive or hope to serve.
4. Are there languages, ages, abilities, family situations, or community realities the site must account for?
5. What are the two or three most important actions a visitor should take?
6. If the whole site could emphasize only one next step, what should it be?
7. What should existing members use the site for rather than another system or app?
8. What should the website deliberately not try to do?

### 3. Identity in plain language

1. If someone asked, “What kind of church is this?” how would you answer without using insider language?
2. What makes this congregation meaningfully itself—not merely different for marketing purposes?
3. What do you want a newcomer to understand about you within ten seconds?
4. What do people often misunderstand about the church?
5. What do regular attenders value most here?
6. What three words should describe the experience of the website?
7. What three words should never describe it?

### 4. Mission, vision, and values

1. What is the church's approved mission statement?
2. What does that statement mean in ordinary language?
3. What is the church seeking to become or accomplish over the next several years?
4. What values guide how the church makes decisions and treats people?
5. For each value, tell a real example of it in practice.
6. Which statements are formally approved, and which are informal descriptions?
7. Are any of these under review or likely to change?

### 5. Story and present season

1. Why and when was the church started?
2. Which moments most shaped what the church is today?
3. What details matter to long-time members?
4. What details would help a newcomer understand the church?
5. How would you describe the church's present season?
6. Are there difficult parts of the story that require careful or private handling?
7. What historical documents or photographs exist, and may they be used publicly?

### 6. Beliefs and ministry philosophy

1. Is there an approved statement of faith? Which version is authoritative?
2. Is the church affiliated with a denomination, network, or tradition? How should that relationship be described?
3. Which beliefs are essential to communicate online?
4. Which theological issues require exact approved wording?
5. How do these beliefs shape worship, discipleship, membership, baptism, communion, leadership, care, and mission?
6. Should the website publish the full statement, a summary, a link, or some combination?
7. Who must approve any edited or summarized theological language?

Never ask AI to synthesize a missing doctrine from sermons or denominational assumptions. Flag the gap for the theology approver.

### 7. Voice and language

1. How should the church sound: conversational, pastoral, formal, energetic, reflective, direct, or something else?
2. Should the writing use “we,” the church name, or both?
3. Which church terms are important, and which need explanation for outsiders?
4. Are there phrases the church often uses that feel authentically its own?
5. Are there phrases, clichés, or tones to avoid?
6. How should scripture be quoted or referenced, and is there a preferred translation?
7. Are there current materials whose voice is a good model?

## Session B — Visitor experience, ministries, and operations

### 8. Service facts

For each service or campus, capture separately:

1. What is its public name?
2. What day and time does it meet?
3. What is the exact address and entrance?
4. Is it in person, online, or both?
5. Does the schedule change seasonally?
6. What style, language, or congregation does it serve?
7. How long does it normally last?
8. Where is the authoritative schedule maintained?
9. Who must update the website when it changes?

### 9. The first-visit journey

Ask the representative to narrate a real visit from beginning to end.

1. How does someone approach the property and know where to go?
2. Where should they park, and are accessible spaces available?
3. Which entrance should they use?
4. Who greets them, and what happens next?
5. How does kids check-in work? What security or pickup details should parents know?
6. What happens during a typical service, in order?
7. What is the music or worship style?
8. What do people usually wear?
9. Will a visitor be asked to stand, speak, give, fill out a card, or identify themselves?
10. Is communion offered, and is guidance needed for guests?
11. What accessibility support is available: ramps, elevators, restrooms, hearing assistance, interpretation, sensory accommodations, or quiet rooms?
12. What happens after the service?
13. What is the simplest next step for someone who wants help or connection?
14. What concerns do visitors commonly have that we have not addressed?

### 10. Children, youth, and vulnerable people

1. Which programs operate during each service?
2. What ages or grades do they serve?
3. Where and when do families check in?
4. What may be said publicly about safety and screening?
5. What information or photos must remain private?
6. Are there allergy, accessibility, pickup, or custody procedures visitors should know?
7. Who receives questions from parents or guardians?

### 11. Ministries and groups

Repeat these questions for each significant ministry:

1. What is the ministry called?
2. Who is it for?
3. Why does it exist?
4. What actually happens?
5. When and where does it meet?
6. Is it ongoing, seasonal, or currently inactive?
7. What should a person do next: visit, register, contact, apply, serve, or give?
8. Is there a cost, prerequisite, capacity limit, or safety restriction?
9. Who leads it, and what contact information may be public?
10. Which system or person maintains the current details?
11. Does it need its own page, or is a short listing enough?

### 12. Next steps and belonging

1. How does someone move from first visit to meaningful connection?
2. How do people learn about membership, baptism, small groups, classes, serving, pastoral care, or giving?
3. Which steps are formal processes, and which are informal invitations?
4. What sequence, if any, should the website recommend?
5. Which next steps require forms or external systems?
6. Who follows up, and what expectation should be set?

### 13. Leadership and governance

1. Which leaders should appear publicly and why?
2. For each leader, what name, title, role, biography, photo, and contact details are approved?
3. How is the church governed, and how much should be explained online?
4. Are any leadership roles vacant or changing?
5. Who approves biographies and titles?

### 14. Sermons, livestream, and media

1. Where are sermons and livestreams currently hosted?
2. Which source is authoritative?
3. How much history should be available?
4. How should messages be organized: date, series, speaker, scripture, or topic?
5. Are transcripts, notes, podcasts, or downloads available?
6. Is a separate Church Online experience needed?
7. What music licensing, privacy, or child-photo restrictions apply?
8. Who publishes, categorizes, and checks new media?

### 15. Events and seasonal content

1. Which events are public, and which are internal?
2. Where is the official calendar maintained?
3. Can that system feed or embed into the site?
4. Which events require registration, payment, waivers, or capacity limits?
5. Which recurring events should be treated as ministries rather than announcements?
6. Which seasonal events need dedicated landing pages?
7. Who creates, approves, publishes, and removes event content?

### 16. Giving and stewardship

1. Which giving provider and public link are authoritative?
2. What types of gifts or funds are available?
3. Are recurring gifts, fees, tax statements, or other details important to explain?
4. How does the church describe the purpose and impact of giving?
5. May specific stories or examples be shared? Who approves them?
6. Are volunteering, in-kind gifts, legacy giving, or facility use part of the same story?
7. Who owns the giving page and verifies changes?

### 17. Contact, care, and forms

1. What physical address, mailing address, phone, email, and office hours are public?
2. Are there special parking or office-entrance directions?
3. Which inquiries need separate pathways: general contact, prayer, pastoral care, counseling, benevolence, facility use, weddings, funerals, school, or media?
4. For each form, who receives it and who provides backup coverage?
5. What response time is realistic internally?
6. What sensitive information should a web form never request?
7. What confirmation should the sender receive?
8. Are retention, consent, or privacy notices required?

### 18. Brand, design, and assets

1. What design work already exists: logo, variations, colors, fonts, templates, signage, print pieces, or brand guide?
2. Which elements are mandatory, preferred, outdated, or open to change?
3. If no system exists, is the project authorized to develop one or only to create the website?
4. Show two or three websites or visual references you respond to. What specifically works?
5. Show any references you dislike. What specifically feels wrong?
6. Should the site feel traditional, contemporary, local, multigenerational, quiet, energetic, liturgical, informal, or something else? What evidence supports that?
7. What real photography and video are available?
8. Who owns the files, and do you have permission to publish identifiable adults and children?
9. Are there facilities, neighborhoods, people, practices, or symbols the visuals should represent?
10. Are there visual clichés or stock-image styles to avoid?
11. What accessibility needs must influence color, type, motion, or interaction?
12. Who approves the visual direction?

### 19. Technology and integrations

1. What platform, host, domain, and DNS provider are in use or preferred?
2. Who legally or organizationally owns each account?
3. Who has administrative access? Is there at least one backup administrator?
4. Which systems must connect: church management, forms, giving, events, sermons, livestream, email, text messaging, maps, analytics, or CRM?
5. Which system is the source of truth for each kind of data?
6. Are there budget, staff skill, procurement, or security constraints?
7. What must staff be able to update without a developer?
8. Are cookie consent, privacy, data retention, or regional legal requirements relevant?
9. What backups, software updates, monitoring, and incident response are expected?

### 20. Ownership and maintenance

1. Who is the ongoing website owner?
2. Who owns service facts, staff, ministries, events, sermons, forms, giving, and seasonal pages?
3. How should ministries request a change?
4. Who approves new pages or major changes?
5. What weekly, monthly, quarterly, and annual review is realistic?
6. What happens when a staff member or volunteer leaves?
7. How will outdated content be identified and removed?
8. Who should receive reports about broken links, failed forms, or accessibility problems?

## Closing questions

1. What important subject have we not discussed?
2. What did you expect me to ask that I did not?
3. Which answers today were uncertain or personal opinions rather than approved facts?
4. Which documents, links, or assets have been mentioned but not supplied?
5. Who else must review the resulting DNA documents?

### Closing script

> We will preserve this transcript, extract individual claims into a fact ledger, and organize confirmed material into topic-specific DNA documents. We will not fill missing information or settle contradictions. You will receive an open-question list and the documents that need your approval before public copy or design begins.

## Verification session agenda

Send the ledger exceptions and DNA drafts ahead of time. Use the meeting only for decisions and corrections.

1. Confirm names, titles, addresses, service times, and contact routes.
2. Resolve every `CONTRADICTED` item.
3. Decide or defer every launch-critical `DECISION_REQUIRED` item.
4. Separate present truth from `ASPIRATIONAL` language.
5. Confirm what is `PRIVATE`.
6. Approve mission, values, beliefs, and sensitive ministry wording.
7. Confirm the primary audience, outcomes, CTA, and launch scope.
8. Assign owners and review dates.
